# Experiment Description (C)
## Apparatus (C)
The Stern-Gerlach experimental apparatus consists of the following components:
First, there is an atom source build up with a furnace wich heats a metal (in the specific case silver) to produce a vapor of atoms which are then directed through a small opening to form a beam.

Then, the atom beam passes through a collimator, which narrows the beam and ensures the atoms travel in a straight path toward a magnetic field region which consist of a non-uniform magnetic field, generated by specially shaped magnets.

After passing through the magnetic field, the atoms are detected on a screen which is typically photographic or fluorescent, with the objective of records the positions where the atoms land.

## Angular & Magnetic momenta (L)
Considering the simple (classical) case of an electron, which is thought as a pointlike particle, orbiting around a nucleus. 
We define its orbital angular momentum, L, as the cross product of its position x and momentum p.

Since the electron is a charged particle, its circular motion is essentially a tiny electrical current loop, and, as we know from classical electro-magnetism, this generates a magnetic field, and specifically a magnetic dipole moment, μ. 

This magnetic moment is directly proportional to the angular momentum with the charge-to-mass ratio as proportionality constant. 

The goal of the Stern-Gerlach experiment is to measure the magnetic moment, and so, to indirectly measure the angular momentum. 
We can probe a magnetic moment using an external magnetic field, B.
The interaction energy of a dipole in a field is given by the magnetic Hamiltonian, which is essentially the cosine of the angle between the two vectors. In order to minimize this energy, the dipole will want to align with the field, just like a compass needle.


## Magnetic Interaction (L)
In a uniform field, there would be no net force acting on the electron and its magnetic dipole would just precess around B. 
However, in a non-uniform field, there is a net force which is the negative gradient of the interaction energy.
Since in the SG apparatus the magnets are designed so that the field and its gradient are strongest in one direction, this simplifies the force equation to this, which is directly proportional to the z-component of the magnetic moment.

Since we know the atom's mass and we can measure the time it spends in the magnet from its velocity, we can use the simple equation of motion to relate the final deflection z(t) directly to a measurement of μ_z.

# Historical Context (C)
## Historical Context (C)
At the beginning of the 20th century, classical physics, which had successfully explained most macroscopic phenomena, began to break down when applied to atomic and subatomic systems. Classical models assumed that physical properties were continuous however, experiments started to reveal that this assumption was not correct.

One of the first significant discoveries came from Max Planck in 1900, who proposed that energy is not emitted continuously, but rather in discrete packets, or "quanta." This insight was based on his work on black-body radiation, which classical physics couldn't explain. Planck's idea was revolutionary because it introduced the concept of quantization, a completely new way of thinking about energy.

In 1905, Albert Einstein expanded on Planck's idea explaining the photoelectric effect that demonstrate that light can be understand also in terms of discrete packets, photons, pointing out the particle-wave dualism.

Around the same time, Niels Bohr introduced a model of the atom with quantized energy levels for electron. This model explained the stability of atoms and accounted for the discrete lines observed in atomic spectraâ€”an important experimental observation that classical theories couldn't explain.

Together, these discoveries showed that the atomic world does not follow the smooth, continuous rules of classical physics. Instead, phenomena at the atomic scale are governed by quantized properties. The idea of quantizationâ€”of energy, angular momentum, and other physical propertiesâ€”was a radical departure from classical thought and set the stage for the development of quantum mechanics.


## Quantum Mechanics (L)
To understand this puzzle, we have to fast-forward a few years to the development of the full, formal mathematical framework of Quantum Mechanics

### States (L)
In this new framework, the state of a system is no longer described by position and momentum, but by an abstract vector, which we write using Dirac's notation as the 'ket' ∣ψ⟩. This vector lives in a complex vector space called a Hilbert space.

Just like any 3D vector can be written as a sum of x, y, z components, a quantum state can be written in terms of a 'basis' — a set of complete, orthonormal vectors ∣ei​⟩.

A crucial difference from a classical state, is the validity of the superposition principle, that affirms that any general state ∣ψ⟩ can be expressed as a linear combination of basis states. 
The coefficients, which represent the projection of the state on the elements of the basis, are called probability amplitudes. 

The measurement of a quantum state is a projective process: any measure would cause a collaps into a specific element of the basis, thus breaking the superposition.

We also define the adjoint operator, which is the conjugate transpose of the matrix O_ij, and acts on the bra, which can be thought as a row vector state.

## Focus: extra dof Q&A (L)
So, why do we see two deflected spots?
This means that the angular momentum being measured, whatever it is, cannot be the orbital one. 
The theory of quantum mechanics is correct, but our model of the atom is missing something.

This puzzle was solved in 1925 by George Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit. They proposed that the electron possesses an intrinsic angular momentum. It's not due to its orbit; it's just a fundamental property of the particle itself, like its charge or its mass.
They called this property spin. 

### Intrinsic Angular Momentum (L)
We postulated that it must be an angular momentum, so it must have the same structure and obey the same commutation rules as L.
We can find simultaneous eigenstates of S^2 and S^z​, which we label ∣s,ms​⟩ and determine the spectrum of ℏ2s(s+1) and ℏms​ while the state degeneracy is 2s+1.

### Final Theoretical Review (L)
And this... solves everything.

The total magnetic moment of the atom is the sum of its orbital and spin components. 
(For the silver atom, it turns out l=0, so only the spin mattered).

### Final Outcomes (C)
The number of spots we see on the screen is 2, so if we set the degeneracy to 2, a simple calculation gives s=1/2.
This is a very crucial difference with respect to the angular momentum, which only allowed integer values. Actually spin allows both integer and semi integer values m_s. 

For example the electron is a 'spin-1/2' particle. This means its ms​ quantum number can only have two possible values: ms​=+1/2 ('spin up') and ms​=−1/2 ('spin down'). This is why the beam splits into exactly two spots!

One final twist: to make the deflection magnitude match the experiment, the g-factor for spin had to be 2, not 1 like it was for orbital momentum. This 'anomalous' g-factor was another deep puzzle, which was later explained by Paul Dirac's relativistic quantum theory.
